According to major studies from [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-07-gartner-says-artificial-intelligence-projects-in-infrastructure-and-operations-stall-ahead-of-meaningful-roi-returns) and [MIT](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/), a large number of projects involving Artificial Intelligence fail for a variety of reasons, ranging from poor planning to problems during execution. In this article, I will discuss some of these issues and suggest ways to mitigate them.

I am writing this article in 2026. Today, we have a huge range of tools that use AI to solve problems, from the simplest to the most complex. AI, in general, became widely known through Generative Artificial Intelligence. By 2020, Generative Adversarial Networks were already popular among specialized audiences for replicating image styles. LLMs continued to evolve, and then came ChatGPT. From that point on, the big boom began. Generative AI became practically synonymous with AI itself, and suddenly every startup pitch adopted AI as part of its vocabulary.

[Pitching the use of AI, "It's AI-powered."](../../../../public/images/ai-fails/ai-powered.png)

At the same time that Generative AI introduced new ways to solve problems, it created an enormous wave of demand for something that did not even have a clear place within many companies. The first major question became: where does AI belong within the organization? In some companies, it became the responsibility of the data team; in others, IT took ownership; and in less structured organizations, it ended up in the hands of the company's hidden department, Shadow IT.

This creates one of the first problems. With so many tools available to solve everyday problems, why not use them to help us with our work? The answer is quite simple: because we can lose control over one of the company's most valuable assets, its data. Using these tools without proper governance means handing company data to third parties without knowing, or having guarantees about, how that data will be used.

At this point, some companies have understood that Generative AI is not simply the topic of the moment or another buzzword, but rather something that is changing the way we perform our tasks, even with the growing concerns surrounding an AI bubble. But that is a discussion for another time. Where we are today, there is no going back. Refusing to adopt AI means giving competitors room to move ahead. The real differentiator will be using it with a well-defined strategy that **does not depend on a single vendor**.

[Robot working on company processes.](../../../../public/images/ai-fails/ai-working.png)

The race to adopt AI within companies began with two main goals: attracting the attention of the market and investors, and improving performance by evolving internal processes. A new technology was suddenly handed over to technical departments that were not always prepared to use it. Top-down decisions in companies with little maturity, combined with pressure from leadership to adopt AI when there is barely a defined use case, create the perfect formula for chaos, which often leads to the same result: project failure.

[Companies racing to adopt AI, represented by robots competing in a horse race.](../../../../public/images/ai-fails/ai-race.png)

Other companies manage to adopt AI but lack the maturity required to sustain their projects, and failures begin to emerge later in the process. These failures are not exclusive to AI projects. In fact, they often expose weaknesses that already exist within the company's internal processes.

One example is the inability to predict usage. Teams may struggle to estimate who will use the solution, how many users it will have, what their average usage will look like, or even what the overall costs will be in order to properly evaluate the cost-benefit relationship. AI can certainly make work and processes easier, but it can also introduce significant costs, potentially making the project itself financially unviable.

Good planning and well-structured processes do not guarantee that projects will succeed, because external factors beyond development can always affect the outcome. However, they help organizations better understand the project's value and avoid premature decisions.

According to the MIT report mentioned earlier, another major reason AI projects fail is how well the tool adapts to its intended audience. A robust solution can be developed, yet adoption may never happen among the people who are actually supposed to use it. One of the main causes is poor communication, or a complete lack of communication, between business and technical teams.

According to Gartner, in addition to the issues mentioned earlier, two other points are particularly delicate in AI projects: **Unrealistic Expectations** and **Poor Data Quality**. Unrealistic expectations may be a direct consequence of the moment we are living through. Every day, we see new models, tools, and agents presented as capable of solving increasingly complex problems. A controlled demonstration works perfectly, a short video looks impressive, and the question quickly follows: why can't we do this inside our company?

The problem begins when we confuse a demonstration with a production-ready solution. Between a working prototype and an application used daily by hundreds or thousands of users lies a long path involving security, governance, observability, availability, integration with existing systems, cost control, and many other factors that are rarely visible in a demonstration.

There is also an expectation that AI should be able to solve problems that the company itself has not yet managed to properly structure. If a process is bad, adding AI will not necessarily make it better. In some cases, we may simply automate a bad process and allow it to execute its problems faster. In that situation, we are only amplifying the problem instead of helping to solve it.

The second point is poor data quality. We can use the best model available, build a robust architecture, and invest heavily in infrastructure, but if the data provided to the solution is poor, it will be very difficult to achieve good results. This problem becomes even more evident in applications that use RAG, agents, or other approaches to provide context to models. There is little value in debating the best model, context window size, or search strategy if the documents are outdated, duplicated, poorly structured, or simply do not contain the information needed to answer the question.

AI does not automatically fix data problems. Often, it simply makes those problems more visible. And perhaps this is one of the most important points about AI projects: **not every problem needs AI**.

With the popularization of the technology, we have started looking for places where AI can be used before we even understand the problem we are trying to solve. The order should be exactly the opposite. First, we understand the problem. Then, we evaluate which technologies can solve it. Only after that should we decide whether AI actually makes sense.

A simple project that automates a small and repetitive task, but has clearly defined users, metrics, and returns, can generate far more value than a massive AI platform created simply because the company needed to show the market that it was using Artificial Intelligence.

## So, how can we prevent AI projects from failing?

There is no formula that guarantees the success of a project, but we can significantly reduce the chances of failure by starting with the basics: understanding the problem we are trying to solve.

Before choosing the model, vendor, or architecture, we need to understand who will use the solution, what problem it solves, what data will be required, how much we are willing to spend, and, most importantly, how we will measure whether it actually delivers value.

We also need to bring technical and business teams closer together. An AI project should not belong exclusively to IT, the data team, or an executive department. The people building the solution need to understand the problem, and the people who own the problem need to participate in the development and validation of the solution.

[AI and people working together to solve problems.](../../../../public/images/ai-fails/worker-ai.png)

Starting small also helps. A pilot project does not need to solve every problem in the company. It needs to answer whether a hypothesis makes sense. If it works, we evolve it. If it does not, we learn at a controlled cost.

And we need to accept that some projects will fail.

Failing in a small experiment because we discovered that a technology does not solve a particular problem is not necessarily a bad outcome. The real problem is investing months of development, infrastructure, and resources into a solution without first defining what success would actually look like.

In the end, AI projects may not fail for reasons that are so different from other technology projects.

They fail because of poor planning, communication problems, poor data quality, unrealistic expectations, adoption difficulties, lack of metrics, and decisions made before fully understanding the problem.

The difference is that the current speed of AI evolution amplifies all of these issues.

The technology changes rapidly, new tools emerge every day, and there is enormous pressure not to fall behind. But being part of this race does not necessarily mean running faster.

It means knowing where we are running.
